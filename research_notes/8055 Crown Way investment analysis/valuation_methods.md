## Key Question 1: Repeat-sales index method (Case-Shiller, FHFA) and how to apply an index projection to one property; hedonic/AVM approaches and their error rates

### Takeaway
A repeat-sales index projects a single home's value as V_T = V_0 x (Index_T / Index_0), but the index explains very little of an individual home's price change (Case and Shiller found R-squared of at most 0.04 and an individual-home annual error of about 14% in Atlanta), so any single-property forecast must carry a wide idiosyncratic band on top of index risk. Current AVM error is small for listed homes (Redfin: 1.90% national, 1.66% Georgia, about 1.9% in Clayton County GA) and much larger off-market (7.46% national, 7.01% Georgia, about 7.6% Clayton County GA); the Zestimate state/metro accuracy table could not be retrieved.

## Key Question 2: Empirical house-price dynamics (momentum, mean reversion to fundamentals) and the predictability of appreciation from rent yield

### Takeaway
Annual house-price changes show strong one-year momentum (autocorrelation about 0.2-0.5 in Case and Shiller's unsmoothed data, 0.67-0.71 in FHFA metro panels) followed by mean reversion at 3-5 year horizons (coefficients about -0.1 to -0.3 on the prior five-year change), and prices revert toward rents (Gallin: a rent-price ratio 10 percentage points above trend predicts real price growth about 1.7 pp/yr faster over the next three years). The local series match: annual AR(1) is 0.54-0.72 for Atlanta/Clayton/30238 and the ZIP's rent yield is now near the top of its 2016-2026 range.

### Cited Findings
- Case and Shiller (1989): "A city-wide real log price index change in one year tends to be followed by a change in the same direction (and between a quarter to a half as large in magnitude) in the subsequent year"; split-sample regressions (Table 2, L=4) give lagged-year coefficients of 0.215 and 0.191 for Atlanta (t about 1.0), 0.412-0.502 for Chicago, 0.254-0.312 for Dallas, 0.255-0.430 for San Francisco — [NBER w2506 PDF](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w2506/w2506.pdf)
- Glaeser and Gyourko (NBER w12787, 2006; published with Morales and Nathanson as "Housing Dynamics: An Urban Approach," JUE 2014): in OFHEO metro data, "Our one year estimate of price change serial correlation is 0.71" (Arellano-Bond), "a $1 increase in housing prices between time t and t+1 is associated with a 71 cent increase between time t+1 and t+2"; the five-year mean-reversion coefficient is -0.32 (-0.20 in logs); plain OLS gives 0.28, 0.08 and -0.51 at one-, three- and five-year horizons; their rational model "readily explains the mean reversion of prices over five year periods, but cannot explain the observed positive serial correlation at higher frequencies" — [NBER w12787 PDF](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w12787/w12787.pdf)
- Capozza, Hendershott, Mack and Mayer (NBER w9262, 2002; published as Capozza, Hendershott and Mack, Real Estate Economics 2004): panel of 62 metros 1979-1995; price change model dP_t = alpha dP_{t-1} + beta (P*_{t-1} - P_{t-1}) + gamma dP*_t; estimates alpha (serial correlation) = 0.33, beta (mean reversion to fundamental value) = 0.25 ("actual prices converge only 25 percent of this difference every year"), gamma (partial adjustment) = 0.52; with repeat-sales data alpha = 0.55 and beta = 0.15; the average city's autocorrelation is 0.49, versus 0.23 for a city with low growth and low real construction costs and 0.75 for a 4%-growth, high-cost city; fundamental value regression loads 0.45 on log real median income, 0.07 on log population, -0.04 on log user cost, -0.38 on a land-supply index; simulated maximum overshoot of 24% when gamma = alpha = 0.75 and beta = 0.1, under 3% when gamma = alpha = 0.5 — [NBER w9262 PDF](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w9262/w9262.pdf)
- Glaeser and Nathanson (NBER w21037, 2015; JFE 2017): empirical autocorrelations of annual FHFA house price changes (panel of 100 cities, 30 years): lag 1 = 0.67, lag 2 = 0.26, lag 3 = -0.10, lag 4 = -0.26, lag 5 = -0.28, lag 6 = -0.31, lag 7 = -0.35, lag 8 = -0.39, lag 9 = -0.34, lag 10 = -0.26; a model of naive extrapolators reproduces momentum (0.75 at lag 1) and reversion, while rational models give near-zero autocorrelation (0.11) — [NBER w21037 PDF](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w21037/w21037.pdf)
- Gao, Sockin and Xiong (NBER w26457, 2019; "Economic Consequences of Housing Speculation," RFS 2020): instrumenting ZIP-level non-owner-occupied purchase share with state capital-gains taxes, a one-standard-deviation (9.9 percentage point) higher non-owner-occupied share in 2004-2006 "led to a housing price increase of 26.5% during the boom, and a drop of 37.4% during the bust," plus +8.4% employment in the boom and -14.6% in the bust; speculation was "partly driven by extrapolation of past price changes" — [NBER w26457](https://www.nber.org/papers/w26457); [PDF](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w26457/w26457.pdf)
- Gallin (FEDS 2004-50, "The Long-Run Relationship between House Prices and Rents"; national data 1970:Q1-2003:Q4): long-horizon regressions of three-year-ahead changes give a coefficient of 0.169 (s.e. 0.038, R-squared 0.14, annual rate) on the log rent-price ratio in the price equation and -0.061 (s.e. 0.017) in the rent equation: "for each 10 percentage point difference between rents and prices, the change in real rents is 0.61 percentage point less per year, and the change in real prices is on average 1.69 percentage points more per year, during the subsequent three years"; bootstrap tests reject the view that rents "do all the correcting" — [FEDS 2004-50 PDF](https://www.federalreserve.gov/pubs/feds/2004/200450/200450pap.pdf)
- Gallin (FEDS 2003-17): in a panel of 95 metros, house prices and per-capita income are not cointegrated, so the common error-correction specification linking prices to income "may be inappropriate" — [FEDS 2003-17 PDF](https://www.federalreserve.gov/pubs/feds/2003/200317/200317pap.pdf)
- Campbell, Davis, Gallin and Martin (FEDS 2006-29; JUE 2009) decompose the rent-price ratio with a dynamic Gordon-growth model for the U.S., four regions and 23 metros (1975-2005): expected real rent growth explains a small share of variation; real rates and housing premia explain most; "65 percent of the decline in the aggregate rent-price ratio [1997-2005] is due to a declining housing premium"; the Atlanta rows of their decomposition tables report mean rent-price ratio values of 5.62 (full sample), 5.73 (1975-96) and 5.33 (1997-2005), in percent (column interpretation by the author) — [FEDS 2006-29 PDF](https://www.federalreserve.gov/pubs/feds/2006/200629/200629pap.pdf)
- Himmelberg, Mayer and Sinai (NBER w11643, 2005; JEP): annual user cost u = r_rf + property tax rate - tax savings x (mortgage rate + property tax rate) + maintenance (delta) - expected capital gain (g) + risk premium (gamma); the equilibrium price-to-rent ratio equals 1/u; Atlanta's average user cost 1980-2004 was 5.3% and 3.9% in 2004 (-31% from its prior peak); Atlanta's 2004 price-to-rent ratio was 1.15 times its 24-year average and price-to-income 1.07 times — [NBER w11643 PDF](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w11643/w11643.pdf)
- Author computation of annual (year-end to year-end) log-change AR(1) coefficients: Zillow ZHVI 30238 2001-2026 = 0.644 (mean 2.82%/yr, sd 15.63%); Clayton County = 0.684 (sd 15.18%); Atlanta MSA = 0.718 (sd 7.86%); U.S. = 0.754 (sd 5.80%); Case-Shiller Atlanta 1992-2026 = 0.542 (mean 3.63%, sd 6.50%); FHFA Clayton County 1976-2025 = 0.607 (sd 7.80%); FHFA Atlanta MSAD 1977-2026 = 0.652 (sd 5.04%); FHFA ZIP5 30238 1981-2025 = 0.596 (mean 3.20%, sd 9.44%) — sources: [Zillow ZHVI ZIP](https://files.zillowstatic.com/research/public_csvs/zhvi/Zip_zhvi_uc_sfrcondo_tier_0.33_0.67_sm_sa_month.csv), [FRED ATXRSA](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/ATXRSA), [FRED ATNHPIUS13063A](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/ATNHPIUS13063A), [FHFA metro file](https://www.fhfa.gov/hpi/download/quarterly_datasets/hpi_at_metro.csv), [FHFA ZIP5 file](https://www.fhfa.gov/hpi/download/annual/hpi_at_zip5.xlsx)
- Author computation of the slope of the forward five-year log change on the past five-year log change (overlapping annual observations, crude): 30238 -0.001 (n=17); Atlanta ZHVI +0.042; U.S. ZHVI -0.281; Case-Shiller Atlanta -0.018 (n=26); Case-Shiller U.S. -0.282 (n=30); FHFA Clayton +0.172 (n=41); FHFA Atlanta +0.095 (n=41) — same sources as above

### Inferences
- Recommended dynamic parameters for a ZIP-level annual model: rho_1 (one-year momentum) 0.60-0.70 with lag-2 about 0.25 and lags 3-5 about -0.1 to -0.3 (Glaeser-Nathanson pattern; local AR(1) 0.60-0.68 confirms); mean reversion to a rent-based fundamental at 0.15-0.25 per year (Capozza beta; Gallin's 0.169 at a three-year horizon).
- Momentum alone currently points down: with mu = 3%, rho = 0.65 and the last 12-month ZIP change of -3.87% (log), E[next 12 months] = mu(1-rho) + rho x (-3.87%) = -1.5% (author illustration), consistent with Zillow's -0.5% ZIP forecast (Key Question 6).
- Rent-fundamental reversion currently points up: the ZIP's log rent-price ratio is 0.121 above its 2016-2026 mean, which under Gallin's coefficient implies real price growth about 2.0 pp/yr above average over the next three years (author illustration; Gallin's estimate is national, 1970-2003, so this is a direction-and-magnitude cue, not a ZIP-specific estimate). The two signals offset in 2027 and favor recovery in 2028-2030.
- Capozza et al.'s cross-section (low real construction cost, moderate growth) predicts lower serial correlation and faster mean reversion for Clayton County-type markets than for coastal markets; empirically the ZIP's annual volatility (sd 9-16%) is far higher than the metro's (5-8%), with roughly double the metro's sensitivity (see Key Question 5).

## Key Question 3: Monte Carlo and scenario practice for a single asset; volatility assumptions; bull/base/bear paths; leverage

### Takeaway
Practitioner scenario anchors exist at the national level (Fed 2026 baseline: house prices roughly flat, +1.2% from Q1 2026 to Q4 2028; severely adverse: -30% to a Q4 2027 trough), and the local history gives the volatility inputs: Atlanta-metro annual sd 5-8% versus 9-16% for ZIP 30238, whose worst five-year outcome since 1980 was -54% to -63% and whose share of negative five-year windows is 20-27%. At 75-80% LTV, every 1% price move is a 4-5% move in equity.

### Cited Findings
- Federal Reserve 2026 supervisory scenarios (published Feb 4, 2026). ### Inferences
- A defensible Monte Carlo for the subject: simulate the Atlanta metro annual log return with mean 3.0-4.5% and sd 5-8% (Case-Shiller/FHFA/Zillow histories), AR(1) 0.55-0.70 with the Glaeser-Nathanson lag structure; map to the ZIP with beta about 1.8 plus an idiosyncratic ZIP shock of sd about 5.6%; add a property-level shock (sd up to about 14% per Case-Shiller's individual-home residual, lower if the AVM band is trusted). A 10-year simulated ZIP path should reproduce a 20-27% probability of a negative five-year window.
- Scenario set grounded in the sources: Bear = Fed severely adverse scaled by the ZIP beta (national -30% implies roughly -45% to -55% for 30238, matching the 2006-2012 experience of -55% FHFA / -65% Zillow); Base = Zillow/Cotality/Fed-baseline flat-to-slightly-down 2027 (-0.5% to -1%), then reversion to the long-run nominal trend of about 3%/yr for the ZIP (FHFA 1980-2025 CAGR 3.25%; Zillow 2000-2026 2.86%); Bull = rates falling to the Zillow 6.5% path or lower with the 2013-2015-type rebound (ZIP annual gains of 14-28% followed the 2012 trough).
- Leverage: equity return multiple = 1 / (1 - LTV) = 4x at 75% LTV and 5x at 80% LTV, so the ZIP's historical one-year 5th-percentile move (-23%) is a -92% to -115% equity outcome before cash flow, while its 95th percentile (+26%) is +105% to +130%.
